Theme

Learn ConjureOS's design system in one page.

This is @conjureos/ui: the CSS every app in ConjureOS should reach for so it looks native, adapts to all nine palettes automatically, and stays accessible without anyone having to think about it per-component. The one-sentence mental model

Everything you see is one palette, resolved through CSS custom properties, controlled by two HTML attributes. Learn those two attributes and you understand the whole system; everything else in this page is detail.

Two independent attributes, both optional, both set on any element (usually <html>):

data-theme
Which palette. One of nine: cnj hal fal win spr sum xms est cnd. Omit it and you get Conjure, the neutral default.
data-flavor
Which end of that palette. dark or light. Omit it and the browser's own light/dark preference decides.

Set one, both, or neither, on any element in your page, not just the root. A descendant that sets its own data-theme switches just that subtree; everything under it that does not set its own value inherits the closer one. There is no cascade fight and no JavaScript required to make that work; it falls straight out of how CSS custom properties already inherit. The Contract section below shows this live.

You never write a colour. You write a role: "this is the lead hue," "this is secondary text," "this is a hairline border." The system resolves the role into an actual value based on whatever theme and flavor are active at that point in the tree. Change the picker above and watch it happen to this whole page.

The theming contract

Inheritance, live

Both attributes resolve through normal CSS custom-property inheritance. A subtree that sets its own data-theme or data-flavor overrides everything above it, and everything below it that has no opinion follows along. The box below is real, nested HTML; resize your understanding, not your imagination.

This outer box follows the picker above (no attributes of its own)

I inherit whatever theme and flavor you picked at the top of the page.

<div data-theme="hal">: this subtree is always Halloween, regardless of the picker, because it set its own theme. It still follows the picker's flavor though, since it never set data-flavor.

<div data-flavor="light">: and this innermost box is always Halloween, always light, three levels deep, with zero JavaScript involved anywhere on this page for this box.
Markup
<div> <!-- inherits from the page -->
  <div data-theme="hal"> <!-- locked to Halloween, any flavor -->
    <div data-flavor="light"> <!-- locked to Halloween AND light -->
      ...
    </div>
  </div>
</div>

Why attributes, not classes. A class-based switch needs either a giant theme-specific stylesheet per palette, or JavaScript rewriting every colour on toggle. Attribute-based theming needs neither: every token is declared once as an inert, unresolved pair (--cui-bg-d / --cui-bg-l) that inherits fully intact down the tree, and a single resolution rule, matching any element carrying either attribute, collapses that pair down to the one value the active flavor wants. Setting an attribute deeper in the tree just means a new element now matches that resolution rule too, with its own inherited pair to resolve from.

data-theme="system" is a special value, not a ninth palette: it clears a theme an ancestor set and returns the subtree to Conjure. It is the one value that behaves like a reset rather than an inheritance.

Who decides, when your app runs inside ConjureOS

Three parties can have an opinion, highest wins: the user, through a setting in your own app (always wins); ConjureOS, when your app runs inside the shell and the user has not overridden it; and your app's own default, the theme you picked because it suits the subject. An app running outside ConjureOS simply never hears from level two and falls straight through to its own default; nothing breaks, nothing needs a conditional. Flavor follows the same ladder with one more rung at the bottom: if nobody has an opinion, the browser's own prefers-color-scheme decides. That is the only thing the operating system controls; it never picks a palette.

Apps run in an iframe, and custom properties do not inherit across that boundary, so if your app wants to follow ConjureOS's theme rather than pin its own, it needs to be told, and it needs to listen. See Wiring theming into your app in MODERN_WHIMSY.md for the full protocol and the ConjureTheme helper that implements the app's half of it in one function call.

Common mistakes

What actually goes wrong

Every mistake below shipped for real, more than once, in the ConjureOS shell. Each comparison is live: the ratio shown is computed, right now, from this page's actual rendered colours in whatever theme you have selected. Flip the picker to a few different themes and watch the numbers move, including into and out of passing.

The big one

Painting the fill's label token onto a translucent wash

--cui-on-accent is calibrated against exactly one thing: the fully opaque --cui-accent fill. That is the only surface it was ever tested against. A wash (a color-mix() or rgba() tint of the accent, sitting over a card rather than being the whole button) composites toward whatever it sits on top of, so its effective colour is never the one --cui-on-accent promises. This single pattern explained nearly every "this theme looks wrong" report across a full nine-theme review of the ConjureOS shell.

The rule that holds: if the background is a wash, the foreground is a TEXT-weight token (--cui-link, --cui-support-text, --cui-third-text). If the background is the opaque fill itself, the foreground is the matching ON token (--cui-on-accent, --cui-onstatus). Never cross the two.

Markup
<!-- Wrong -->
<div style="background: color-mix(in srgb, var(--cui-accent) 30%, transparent);
             color: var(--cui-on-accent)">Active tab</div>

<!-- Right -->
<div style="background: color-mix(in srgb, var(--cui-accent) 30%, transparent);
             color: var(--cui-link)">Active tab</div>
Hunting the wrong thing

Searching for a known bad value instead of the shape of the bug

Grepping for a specific hex you already know is bad (say, a retired brand colour) only proves that one value is gone. Real cleanups in this project found new, previously unknown hardcoded colours on the second, third, and fourth pass over the same file, each time a value-specific search had already declared victory. What actually catches every instance: search by shape, not value.

The grep that actually works
Any #hex or rgb(/rgba( literal sitting inside a
background, border, or color declaration
that is NOT wrapped in var() or color-mix().

A single low-opacity literal buried in the 70% stop of a 4-stop animated gradient is nearly invisible on visual review, and survives visual review after visual review. It is trivial to catch structurally.

Wrong tier

Using "third" as a dominant colour instead of a sparing note

--cui-third exists for a tag, a secondary stat, one accent on one card in a grid, it is documented as "use sparingly" for exactly this reason. Painting it as the background of something that renders constantly and everywhere, every app icon on a desktop, say, makes the theme's third hue read as the app's whole identity. On some palettes that third hue has nothing to do with the theme's lead at all, so a treatment meant to look "on brand" ends up looking arbitrary instead.

If a treatment needs to run on every instance of something, build it from --cui-accent (a soft tint of it, not the solid fill), never from --cui-support or --cui-third.

Composition

Layering two translucent hues that are far apart

A gradient or overlapping wash blending --cui-accent and --cui-support looks fine on the themes where those two hues sit close together in hue angle, and turns to grey mud on the themes where they sit near-complementary (Summer and Candyland especially). Test a multi-hue composite against the theme with the widest hue gap between its two colours, not just against whichever theme you happened to have open.

Live on the current theme

Switch the picker to Summer or Candyland and watch the middle of that bar; on most other themes it stays clean. The fix used throughout this system is either a pre-composited opaque tint (bake the blend once, ship the result, no live alpha compositing) or, more often, simply not blending two hues at all: a soft tint of ONE hue reads as "the theme" far more reliably than a blend of two.

Fill vs. text

Using a fill-weight token as text, or vice versa

--cui-accent and --cui-link are the same hue and deliberately different values. This is the single most load-bearing idea in the whole token set: a colour bright enough to read as text on a dark ground is usually too light to carry a white label as a fill, and a colour dark enough to carry a white label is usually too dark to read as text on its own. Using --cui-accent as a text colour is often still legible, which is exactly what makes the mistake easy to miss in review: it passes on the one theme you tested and fails on three others where the lead hue was tuned bright specifically to work as a fill, not as text.

Palette design rules

If you are designing a theme of your own

Useful if you are building a tenth palette, or wondering why a colour you expected is not there. Two of the nine rules got a full live demonstration in Common mistakes above (one hue per job, complementary hues cannot composite); the rest are here.

Warm dark grounds have a mud band

At Lab hue 60-85 (the warm-brown range), a dark surface with chroma above roughly 5.5 stops reading as "warm dark" and starts reading as mud. Fall's ground sits deliberately under that line; darkening a muddy colour further does not rescue it, because this is a chroma limit, not a lightness limit.

The ground's own chroma decides whether a coloured card can exist on it

Not the hue distance between the card and the ground, and not their overall colour distance: the chroma of the ground itself. Below roughly 4, a ground asserts no hue of its own and any coloured card reads cleanly against it. Above that, the ground starts competing, and a card has to move further away in value than a season's palette usually allows.

Bright colours are fills in light flavors, never text

A colour bright enough to feel seasonal on a white-ish ground cannot also be legible as text on it. Light flavors keep the bright value for fills and pair it with a separately deepened value for text, which is why --cui-support and --cui-support-text diverge sharply on light flavors and barely at all on dark ones. Pick any theme above and flip to light; watch the support swatch in the token reference stay bright while the support-text swatch goes noticeably darker.

Contrast is checked, not eyeballed

Every one of the eighteen theme × flavor sets was computed against WCAG 2.1: 4.5:1 for body text, 3:1 for interactive fills and focus rings per success criterion 1.4.11. Several palettes that looked correct on screen failed the actual math and were retuned. Use the checker above rather than trusting your eyes, especially for anything under 16px or any colour-on-colour pairing that is not plain text on a plain ground.
